**Topic 2: COBOL Basics — Research Summary**

**What is COBOL, and who introduced it?**

COBOL stands for Common Business-Oriented Language. It is a verbose, easy-to-read programming language that was developed in 1959 and used in mainframe computing systems. It handles things like credit card transactions and airline ticketing systems. COBOL is a high-level, imperative, and procedural programming language. It contains over 600 reserved words, and every COBOL program follows an easy-to-understand hierarchy, starting with programs, divisions, sections, paragraphs, sentences, statements, and characters.

COBOL also uses a fixed-column format with specific rules. The first six columns are used for line numbers, while column seven is used to identify comments. Columns 12–72 are where you write the actual code. The reason the code doesn't go beyond column 72 is that COBOL was originally designed around physical punch cards. Periods are also used to end sentences in COBOL, which contributes to its readable structure.

**How was it first integrated into infrastructure (mainframes, government, banks)?**

COBOL was first integrated into infrastructure during the 1960s through mainframe computers used by governments, banks, and businesses. The U.S. Department of Defense supported COBOL because it wanted a programming language that could work across different computer systems. Banks later adopted COBOL to process financial transactions, manage customer records, and automate accounting. Over time, COBOL became an important part of their infrastructure because these organizations continued depending on the systems they had built.

**How many systems use COBOL today?**

COBOL programs are still used globally by governments and various industries, including retail, travel, finance, and healthcare.

**What is the value of the systems that run on COBOL (transactions, money, records)?**

The value of COBOL systems is the money they help process, the financial transactions they handle, and the important records they maintain for banks, governments, and other industries.

**What is the average age of COBOL engineers, and why does it matter?**

There isn't a reliable current average age for COBOL engineers, but older surveys suggest that many experienced COBOL programmers were 45 years old or older. This matters because as these engineers retire, banks and governments can lose people who understand and maintain their legacy systems. Finding replacements can be difficult because COBOL is not as commonly taught as newer programming languages.

Source: https://www.computerworld.com/article/1528379/where-cobol-systems-go-from-here.html?utm_source

**How much effort would replacing COBOL take, and why have so many replacement projects struggled?**

Replacing COBOL systems can take years and cost millions of dollars because organizations have to rebuild older applications, transfer important records, and make sure transactions continue working correctly. Many replacement projects struggle because of high costs, complicated business rules, a shortage of experienced engineers, and the risk of disrupting important services. Banks and governments have to carefully test new systems before replacing the old ones.

**Lab hook: In payments.dat, 125.50 is stored as 000000012550. Why would a 1959 business language store money with an implied decimal instead of a decimal point?**

COBOL uses an implied decimal because it was designed to process financial records using fixed-width fields. Instead of storing a decimal point, the program already knows that the last two digits represent cents. For example, $125.50 is stored as `000000012550`. This saves space, keeps records consistent, and allows money to be calculated accurately without relying on floating-point approximations.

Topic 5: How Everything Connects

Question 1: How does a payment move through the Balerica banking system?

In the Balerica banking system, a payment can be understood as moving through several different technologies. First, a SWIFT message contains instructions about a payment between financial institutions. FastAPI acts as the modern interface that receives a payment request and connects it to the backend. The COBOL program processes the transaction using the banking rules, and fixed-width files help organize information into a format the legacy program understands. SQLite is used to store and retrieve banking data. COBOL and fixed-width files represent the older technology, while FastAPI is the modern part of the application. Banks keep both because their existing systems may already handle important transactions reliably, while modern APIs allow newer applications to communicate with them.

Question 2: Why do organizations wrap legacy systems with APIs instead of replacing them?

Organizations often keep their legacy systems because replacing everything at once can be expensive, complicated, and risky. For example, banks may have COBOL programs that have been processing transactions for decades. Instead of rebuilding the entire system, they can create an API that allows modern applications to communicate with the older software. This gives the organization time to modernize gradually without interrupting important services. However, the older system still needs maintenance and security improvements because adding an API does not automatically fix its existing problems.

Question 3: How do these technologies matter to different industries?

These technologies are important because different industries need reliable ways to process information, store records, and communicate between systems.

**Financial:** Banks use databases and transaction-processing programs to manage accounts and payments. For example, SWIFT provides standardized messages that banks use to communicate payment instructions internationally. APIs can connect modern banking applications to older transaction systems.

**Medical/Healthcare:** Hospitals use databases to store patient records and APIs to exchange information between healthcare applications. For example, a hospital can use the FHIR API standard to allow authorized software to retrieve patient information from an electronic health record system.

**Academic:** Universities use databases to manage student records, grades, and course registration. For example, a university learning management system can use APIs to exchange enrollment information with its student information system.

**Government:** Government agencies use older computer systems to process important public services. For example, the 2025 GAO report identifies U.S. Treasury systems that use COBOL and assembly language. These systems demonstrate why governments need to maintain existing technology while planning modernization.

Not every industry uses all four technologies. SWIFT is mainly used in financial messaging, while databases and APIs are common across many industries.

Question 4: What risks does the GAO report describe for systems that are decades old?

According to the 2025 GAO report, operating older computer systems creates several risks for government agencies. The report identified 11 critical federal legacy systems that needed modernization, including 8 that used outdated programming languages, 4 that had unsupported hardware or software, and 7 that had known cybersecurity vulnerabilities. These problems can make systems more expensive to maintain and harder to protect against cyberattacks. Another problem is that fewer engineers may understand older programming languages such as COBOL and assembly language. If organizations fail to modernize these systems carefully, they could experience security incidents, outages, or disruptions to important services. This helped me understand why modernization requires planning instead of simply replacing software without testing it.

Question 5: How is AI being used to understand or modernize COBOL systems, and what are its limits?

AI is being used to help engineers understand older COBOL programs and modernize them more efficiently. For example, IBM's watsonx Code Assistant for Z can analyze COBOL code, explain what it does, help document existing programs, and assist with transforming parts of COBOL applications into Java. This can help engineers who are unfamiliar with older systems understand the code before making changes. However, AI can still misunderstand business rules, generate incorrect explanations, or produce code that does not behave exactly like the original program. In a banking system, even a small mistake could affect payment processing or account balances. Engineers still need to review the code, test the results, compare system behavior, and consult people who understand the application before making changes in production.
